# Knight Lore Remake — Non-Negotiable Art Direction

This document defines the visual direction for the project. It is **not optional guidance**. All future graphics, animation, room decoration, character work, and rendering decisions must follow this spec.

If a proposed visual change conflicts with this document, **the document wins**.

---

## Purpose

The game must stop looking like a technically correct prototype made from neutral geometric primitives.

The target is **not** generic retro isometric.
The target is **not** clean vector demo art.
The target is **not** realistic medieval rendering.

The target is:

**A grotesque fairy-tale, cursed castle diorama with chunky silhouettes, theatrical atmosphere, and memorable room identity.**

The feel should be:
- strange
- haunted
- playful
- ominous
- hand-authored
- stylized
- readable at a glance

The game should feel like an illustrated nightmare box, not a geometry engine.

---

## Primary Visual Thesis

The project must preserve the strengths of classic Knight Lore:
- readable isometric depth
- room-based puzzle staging
- strong object clarity
- theatrical, mysterious spaces

But the presentation must add what the current implementation lacks:
- personality
- asymmetry
- silhouette appeal
- authored focal points
- emotional atmosphere
- visual hierarchy

**Core rule:**
The renderer is responsible for spatial logic.
The art is responsible for charm, mood, and identity.

---

## Non-Negotiable Principles

### 1. Silhouette first
Every important visual element must be recognizable in flat black silhouette.

This includes:
- player human form
- player werewolf form
- enemies
- important items
- major room props
- puzzle-critical blocks

If a design only reads because of color, it is not good enough.

### 2. Charm over geometric purity
If a shape is technically correct but visually dull, change it.

Prefer:
- exaggerated proportions
- tilted forms
- uneven hems
- bent poses
- oversized props
- compressed or stretched silhouettes

over strict mechanical symmetry.

### 3. Authoring over assembly
Important visuals must not be generated by combining generic rectangles, ovals, and recolors alone.

Important visuals must be **designed**.
That can still be vector-based, but it must be intentional, curated, and silhouette-driven.

### 4. Every room needs a memory hook
A room must not be just “stone walls + floor + blocks”.
Each important room must contain at least one memorable visual anchor.

Examples:
- giant crooked throne
- ritual altar
- chained cauldron
- moonlit barred window
- hanging cage
- diseased banner
- broken monument
- huge root or crack running through the room

### 5. Unevenness is required
Perfect symmetry creates deadness.

The world must contain:
- off-center focal points
- varied clutter density
- asymmetrical costume shapes
- imperfect wear and damage
- selective ornament, not repeated ornament everywhere

### 6. Decorative restraint
Do not fill every surface with detail.
Atmosphere needs contrast.
Some areas must stay quiet so the focal elements feel important.

### 7. Readability before realism
All gameplay-relevant objects must read instantly.
Do not hide interactables inside realistic detail.
Stylization and clarity are more important than physical realism.

---

## Forbidden Outcomes

The art must **not** look like:
- a debug renderer
- a tilemap with fancier colors
- a vector mockup
- a procedural dungeon test scene
- an AI-generated generic retro game
- a polished but emotionally empty mobile demo

The following are explicitly forbidden unless justified by this document:
- plain rectangular torsos and limbs with only color variation
- symmetric costume design on all characters
- identical wall treatment in every room
- all props built from the same box language
- “more dithering” as a substitute for art direction
- “new palette” as a substitute for art direction
- “more texture” as a substitute for composition
- centered focal composition in every room
- recolor-only differentiation between items
- smooth sterile shapes with no attitude

## Character Direction

### Player — Human form
The human player character must feel like a cursed, weary fairy-tale adventurer.

Not a generic explorer.
Not a neutral knight mannequin.

#### Required qualities
- immediately recognizable silhouette
- stooped posture or weighted stance
- oversized headgear or head shape
- heavy upper-body shape
- distinctive lower silhouette, coat tails, tabard, boots, or ragged hem
- one asymmetrical costume detail
- visual sense of burden, mystery, or curse

#### Must read as
- vulnerable but determined
- strange and memorable
- stylized, not proportionally correct

#### Forbidden character traits
- generic heroic upright stance
- plain torso rectangle with attached arms
- fully mirrored silhouette left/right
- “blue shorts”, belts, or trim used as main identity device
- expressionless mannequin posture

### Player — Werewolf form
The werewolf must not read as “the same body but furry”.
It needs a totally different mass distribution.

#### Required qualities
- hunched silhouette
- long forearms or oversized claw mass
- clear snout/head wedge
- visible back arc or shoulder hump
- stronger diagonal energy than human form
- predatory but tragic tone

#### Must read as
- unstable
- cursed
- dangerous
- visually distinct even at small size

#### Forbidden werewolf traits
- upright human proportions with ears added
- same leg and torso proportions as human form
- smooth neutral pose
- dependence on eye color or face detail for readability

### Other actors
Enemies must each have a distinct dominant shape language.

- Guard: upright pillar / rigid authority
- Ghost: tapering drift / floating cloth shape
- Druid: hooked, branch-like, ritual silhouette
- Robot or odd entities: angular relic, not generic block-man

No two enemy classes should share the same body logic with palette swaps.

---

## Item Direction

Items must be readable by silhouette first and color second.

Every collectible requires a unique iconic outline.

### Required item rules
- each item must be identifiable in monochrome silhouette
- item scale may be exaggerated for readability
- props should feel like story objects, not generic pickups
- highlight shape and contour over texture

### Forbidden item rules
- same base geometry with different colors
- tiny detail-dependent identity
- realistic-scale objects that become unreadable in isometric view

Examples:
- goblet must have a dramatic cup silhouette
- vial must have neck and bulb distinction
- crystal ball must read as orb-on-base
- key must have exaggerated bit/teeth shape
- skull must read instantly from outline, not face shading

---

## Room Direction

Each room must feel staged, not merely generated.

### Every important room must contain
- 1 anchor set-piece
- 1 dominant light logic
- 1 area of deep shadow or negative space
- 2 support motifs maximum
- at least 1 relatively quiet wall/floor zone

### Room identity must come from
- focal composition
- silhouette landmarks
- selective decoration
- light placement
- room-specific motifs

### Not enough on their own
The following do **not** create room identity by themselves:
- different stone colors
- different dither density
- extra wall texture
- floor pattern changes only

### Good anchor examples
- crooked throne on a dais
- altar under a shaft of cold light
- chained cauldron with green glow
- massive barred window casting long floor shadows
- gallows-like beam with hanging hooks
- giant tapestry or banner partially torn
- cracked statue or monolith

---

## Material and Surface Rules

Materials must support mood, not dominate the scene.

### Rules
- do not texture every wall equally
- do not apply the same wear pattern uniformly across all surfaces
- blocks, walls, floors, and props must not all share the same edge logic
- one dominant material per room, one accent material, one grime/wear treatment

### Surface hierarchy
Use surfaces to support hierarchy:
- structural surfaces = quieter
- focal props = more distinctive
- gameplay-critical objects = clearest read
- background decoration = more subdued

### Forbidden surface behavior
- every wall equally busy
- every floor equally patterned
- every block equally highlighted
- repeated decorative strokes in every room

---

## Lighting Direction

Lighting is theatrical, not realistic.

The game should use light to create mystery and emphasis.

### Required lighting rules
- every important room has a dominant light idea
- shadows should create shape drama
- light can be stylized and selective
- focal objects should often be framed by contrast

### Acceptable light ideas
- moonbeam from barred window
- torch pools on one wall only
- sickly cauldron underglow
- doorway glow against dark room mass
- altar spotlight in an otherwise dim chamber

### Forbidden lighting behavior
- flat even illumination everywhere
- every wall equally visible
- purely functional lighting with no mood

---

## Animation Direction

Animation must communicate attitude, not just state changes.

### Character motion rules
- idle must show life, tension, burden, or unease
- walk must have rhythm and shape change, not just leg alternation
- jump must compress then release
- landing must feel heavy
- transform must feel uncanny and asymmetrical

### Forbidden motion
- mannequin idle
- identical motion arcs for all actors
- purely linear pose interpolation with no character
- state changes that only recolor or resize parts

---

## Composition Rules

### Per screen / room
There must be a visual hierarchy:
1. focal element
2. gameplay-relevant elements
3. supporting environment
4. quiet/background areas

### Required composition traits
- off-center interest
- directional flow
- framing using light/shadow/architecture
- at least one silhouette that catches the eye immediately

### Forbidden composition traits
- everything centered
- all walls visually equal
- same detail density everywhere
- no quiet space
- no focal contrast

---

## Color Rules

Color is a supporting system, not the main source of identity.

### Rules
- silhouettes and value separation matter more than hue
- room palette should be controlled and limited
- accent colors should be rare and meaningful
- color should help hierarchy, not replace it

### Forbidden color behavior
- relying on palette swaps to create originality
- too many equally saturated accents
- flat mid-tone scenes with weak value contrast
- using “retro colors” without compositional intent

---

## Implementation Mandates

The codebase must evolve toward a split between:
- **procedural structure**
- **authored presentation**

### Procedural systems may continue handling
- room layout
- collision
- depth ordering
- puzzle logic
- actor placement
- transitions

### Authored asset systems must handle
- player forms
- enemy silhouettes
- item silhouettes
- set-piece props
- decorative overlays
- room motifs
- focal composition pieces

### Architectural requirement
`RoomEntityFactory` or equivalent placement code must not remain the main source of visual creativity.
It should place and orchestrate designed assets, not invent all art from primitive geometry.

---

## Review Checklist

Any future graphics PR must be rejected if the answer to any of these is “no”:

- Is the player silhouette memorable in black fill?
- Does the werewolf have a truly distinct body logic?
- Would this room still be memorable without color?
- Does the room have one visual anchor?
- Is there asymmetry in the composition?
- Is there a clear hierarchy of focal vs quiet areas?
- Are important items recognizable by outline?
- Is the change improving personality, not just polish?
- Does this feel hand-authored rather than procedurally assembled?

---

## Final Rule

Do not ask “is this cleaner?” as the main visual question.
Ask:

- is it memorable?
- is it eerie?
- does it have attitude?
- does it read instantly?
- does it feel authored?

If not, it is not done.
